THE LAST KEEPER OF GULL ROCK

Marta Velez arrived on Gull Rock in the first week of March, carrying two suitcases, a crate of books and a letter from the Harbour Board that described her duties in eleven numbered paragraphs. The island was a hump of black granite a mile and a half off the town of Porthcarrow. It had one building worth the name, the lighthouse, and one tree, a stunted hawthorn that leaned away from the west wind as if it were trying to leave. The previous keeper, an old man named Tobias Crane, had died in January. Nobody in Porthcarrow wanted the job, which paid poorly and promised nothing but weather.

The letter told her to light the lamp at sunset and put it out at sunrise, to wind the clockwork that turned the lens every four hours, to record the wind, the sea state and every passing vessel in the log, and to clean the brass and the glass each morning. Paragraph eleven said that the Board expected to review the station at the end of the year. Marta read that paragraph several times. She did not know what a review meant, and the clerk who handed her the letter had not looked her in the eye.

The first weeks were mostly work. The lamp burned paraffin, and the paraffin had to be carried up ninety-seven steps in cans. The clockwork weight dropped down a shaft in the centre of the tower and had to be cranked back to the top with a handle that bit into her palms. She learned to sleep in pieces, two hours here and three hours there, and to wake before the alarm because the lens had begun to slow. She learned the sounds of the tower: the tick of the escapement, the sigh of the wind in the gallery rail, and the dull knock of the sea in the gully below the landing.

Crane had left forty years of logbooks on a shelf in the watch room. Marta began reading them in the evenings. The early books were neat and dull, full of wind directions and ship names. The later books were stranger. In his last winter Crane had started to write about a vessel he called the grey schooner. He saw it, he wrote, on nights of heavy fog, always heading north and always without lights. He reported it to the Harbour Board three times. The Board's replies were folded into the back of the book, and each one was shorter than the last. The final reply asked him to confine his reports to vessels that existed.

In April the supply boat began coming every second Thursday. It was run by a young fisherman named Owen Pascoe, who brought paraffin, flour, tinned fish, the post, and more news than anyone had asked for. Owen told her that the Harbour Board was arguing about money. A company from the city had offered to fit Gull Rock with an electric lamp, a battery bank and a radio beacon that would run without anybody living on the island. The chairman of the Board, a draper called Silas Herne, was in favour. The harbour master, Agnes Polglase, was against it, because she did not trust a machine that could not see.

Marta wrote to her brother Daniel in the city that night. She told him about the stairs and the clockwork and the hawthorn tree, and she told him about the grey schooner, because it made a better story than the paraffin. She did not tell him about paragraph eleven. Daniel answered three weeks later. He worked as a draughtsman for a shipping insurer, and he wrote that the company kept records of every wreck on that coast for the last century. He had looked, out of curiosity, and found a schooner called the Merrow that had gone missing near Gull Rock in the autumn of the year Crane took the job.

The summer was calm. Yachts came out from Porthcarrow on Sundays, and children waved at the tower from the decks. Marta painted the gallery rail, replaced a cracked pane in the lantern and planted potatoes in a bed of seaweed and soil behind the oil store. She kept the log as Crane had kept it in his early years, neat and dull. Owen began staying for tea when he brought the supplies, and then for supper, and once, when the wind got up before he could leave, for the night in the spare room below the watch room.

The fog came in October. For four days the island sat inside a white wall that swallowed the beam a hundred yards from the lens. Marta sounded the fog bell every two minutes by hand, because the mechanism that should have done it had rusted solid years before. On the third night, a little after two in the morning, she heard an engine. It was low and slow, the sound of a heavy vessel feeling its way. She rang the bell harder and kept ringing it until her arms shook. The engine noise came closer, stopped, and then faded away to the north. In the morning there was nothing on the water but a broken fish box.

She wrote it in the log exactly as it happened: an unlit vessel, heard and not seen, heading north. She did not call it a schooner. When Owen came on Thursday he told her that the coastguard had stopped a cargo boat at Pendower that same night. It had been running without lights to avoid the customs men, and it had been carrying brandy and tobacco. Its skipper said he had been lost in the fog until he heard a bell. He said that without the bell he would have put her on the rocks.

Marta understood then that Crane had not been mad. For forty years the fog had been a road for smugglers, who ran north without lights because lights were what the customs men looked for. Crane had seen them, and he had reported them, and the Board had told him to report only vessels that existed. She sent a copy of her log entry to Agnes Polglase, along with Crane's three reports and the Board's three replies, and a note from Daniel about the Merrow.

The review took place in December, in the upstairs room of the Harbour Office. Silas Herne presented the company's offer. The electric lamp would cost less over ten years than a keeper's wages, he said, and it would never fall asleep. Agnes Polglase read out the coastguard report from Pendower and Marta's log entry. Then she asked the company's engineer whether his radio beacon would hear an unlit boat in fog, and whether his battery bank would ring a bell for it. The engineer said that it would not. The Board voted four to three to keep a keeper on Gull Rock for another five years, and to mend the fog bell mechanism at the Board's expense.

Owen took Marta back to the island that afternoon. The sea was grey and quiet, and the hawthorn was still leaning away from the wind. She climbed the ninety-seven steps, wound the clockwork, and lit the lamp a few minutes before sunset. In the log she wrote the wind, the sea state, and one passing vessel: the supply boat, heading home with its lights on.
